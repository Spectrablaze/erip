Demo Motors Ltd

Fuelled by legacy, charged by growth

About the company

Demo Motors Ltd is an Indian multinational two-wheeler manufacturer incorporated in 1982, with a dominant position in the mid-size motorcycle segment. The company manufactures premium motorcycles alongside riding apparel, accessories and lifestyle products, and operates in over 65 countries through 2,000 domestic stores and 1,085 international outlets.

Installed capacity stands at 14 lakh units annually across four plants, supported by technical centres in Chennai and the United Kingdom and seven CKD assembly units across Nepal, Brazil, Thailand, Argentina, Bangladesh and Colombia.

The company also holds a 54% stake in a commercial-vehicle joint venture manufacturing trucks from 2 to 55 tonnes and buses seating 16 to 62 passengers.

Investment thesis

Volume growth of 22.3% in FY26 came almost entirely from the sub-350cc portfolio, which lowered the average customer age from 38–40 to 24–26 years and expanded the addressable base rather than cannibalising it. With capacity at 100% utilisation and a INR 958 crore brownfield expansion taking capacity to 20 lakh units by FY28, the constraint through FY27 is supply, not demand. At 43x trailing earnings against a peer median of 48x, the discount looks unjustified given a 46.5% gross margin and a negative cash conversion cycle.

Global economy

The global economy entered 2026 in stable but uneven condition. Trade tensions, policy uncertainty and long-running structural drags weighed on activity through the first quarter, and the outlook deteriorated sharply once conflict in the Middle East began.

The disruption matters disproportionately because the Strait of Hormuz carries a material share of seaborne crude, LNG, fertiliser and industrial feedstock. Energy prices and freight rates have both moved violently since, and the second-round effects are now visible in headline inflation across importing economies.

What the IMF now expects

On the WEO April 2026 baseline — which assumes the conflict stays contained and conditions normalise by mid-year — global growth is 3.1% in 2026 and 3.2% in 2027, against a 2000–19 average of 3.7%. Advanced economies slow to 1.8%; the US holds up at 2.3% while the euro area decelerates to 1.1%. China steps down from 5.0% to 4.4%.

  • Headline inflation revised up to 4.4% for 2026, stalling the 2024–25 disinflation.
  • Central banks expected to hold restrictive stances longer than previously priced.
  • India remains the fastest-growing major economy at 6.5%.
Read-through: the growth gap between India and every other major economy widens to roughly 340 bps in 2026 — the widest since 2021.

Revenue compounded at 21.9% over FY21–FY26, but the composition of that growth shifted decisively in the last two years. Through FY22–FY23 growth was price-led: realisation rose 18% on a mix shift into 650cc twins while volumes were flat. From FY24 onward the driver inverted.

Volume, not price, now carries the top line

FY26 revenue growth of 24.0% decomposes into 22.3% volume and 1.4% realisation. That is the healthier configuration: it means the sub-350cc launches expanded the customer base rather than trading it down, and it removes the pricing-power question that hung over the FY23 numbers.

Exports contributed 14.5% of revenue against 11.2% two years ago, with the Americas and EMEA each growing above 30%. Management has guided to exports reaching a fifth of revenue by FY28.

Read-through: at 100% capacity utilisation, FY27 volume growth is capped near 8–10% until the Cheyyar brownfield line commissions in H2FY28.

Margin bridge, FY25 to FY26

The 100 bps contraction in EBITDA margin despite 60 bps of gross-margin expansion is entirely a mix and spend story. Advertising rose 34% ahead of the electric launch, and the newer sub-350cc products carry roughly 400 bps lower contribution margin than the 650cc twins.

  • Gross margin +60 bps on softer alloy and steel prices.
  • Advertising and promotion −90 bps.
  • Product mix −70 bps.
  • Operating leverage +40 bps on 22.3% volume growth.

Segment revenue mix

Motorcycles contribute 62% of consolidated revenue. Spares and accessories at 18% are the quiet compounder — they carry gross margins roughly 900 bps above the vehicle business and scale with the installed parc rather than with current-year volume.

Read-through: a 45 lakh unit installed parc growing 12% a year gives the spares line a growth floor independent of the motorcycle cycle.

Management analysis — board of directors

Siddhartha Lal
Executive Chairman · with the group since 1999

Joined the group in 1999 and became CEO in 2000 at the age of 26, when the motorcycle business was loss-making and the group ran nine unrelated divisions. He divested seven of them, concentrated capital on motorcycles and commercial vehicles, and rebuilt the product line around the UCE and J-series platforms. Holds a mechanical engineering degree and an automotive engineering masters from the University of Leeds.

Inder Mohan Singh
Independent Director · appointed 2021

Corporate lawyer with three decades in governance, M&A, private equity and regulatory practice; a Fellow Member of the Institute of Company Secretaries of India. Chairs the audit committee and has attended all eight board meetings in FY26.

Mahesh Tripathi
Chief Operations Officer

Nearly three decades in production and supply-chain management across two-wheeler and component manufacturers. Responsible for the four-plant network and the Cheyyar brownfield expansion now underway.

Mohit Dhar Jayal
Chief Brand Officer

Leads brand strategy, communications and the retail-experience programme. Previously founded and ran an independent creative agency; joined to build the ownership-community programme that now anchors customer retention.

SWOT analysis

Strengths

  • 88% share of the domestic mid-size motorcycle segment.
  • 46.5% gross margin, 640 bps above the nearest peer.
  • Negative cash conversion cycle of 44 days; net cash balance sheet.
  • Brand equity that supports pricing without discounting.

Weaknesses

  • Capacity fully utilised; no headroom before H2FY28.
  • Single-brand, single-segment concentration in the core business.
  • Late entry into electric two-wheelers relative to pure-plays.
  • International volumes still under 15% of the total.

Opportunities

  • Premiumisation of the 150–350cc band as incomes rise.
  • Tier-2 and tier-3 penetration where financing access is improving.
  • Spares and apparel scaling with a 45 lakh unit installed parc.
  • Export expansion into Latin America and South-East Asia.

Threats

  • Japanese and domestic entrants targeting the 350cc segment directly.
  • Commodity and freight inflation from energy-price shocks.
  • Regulatory shifts on emissions and safety raising per-unit cost.
  • EV transition compressing the ICE replacement cycle.

Disclosure checks

  • Auditor unchanged for six years; unmodified opinion, no emphasis of matter.
  • No CFO or auditor change in the last three years.
  • Related-party transactions at 2.1% of revenue, all with the CV joint venture.
  • Contingent liabilities at 3.4% of net worth, largely indirect-tax disputes.
  • Promoter pledging: nil, unchanged for ten years.
  • R&D fully expensed; no interest capitalised outside the Cheyyar project.
  • Other income at 17.9% of PBT — high, and treasury-driven; the operating business is correspondingly less profitable than headline PAT suggests.
Conclusion. Earnings quality is high on every mechanical test. The one item worth monitoring is the rising share of other income in PBT, which reflects a INR 15,000 crore treasury book rather than any accounting choice — but it does mean headline ROE overstates the return on the operating business by roughly 300 bps.

Reading the multiples

The company trades at a 23% discount to the peer median on book value while earning ROE in line with the group, and at an 11% discount on earnings. That is inconsistent with the quality metrics — 640 bps of gross-margin advantage, a negative working-capital cycle and a net-cash balance sheet.

The premium shows up only on EV/Sales, where the 71% gap is a direct function of the margin advantage rather than a valuation stretch. Normalising for margin, EV/Sales sits within 5% of the median.

Read-through: relative valuation says fair-to-cheap, the DCF says expensive. The gap is the treasury book and the capacity expansion — both of which the DCF handles crudely and the multiples ignore entirely.

Investment rationale, risks and verdict

Rationale

  • Volume-led growth has replaced price-led growth. FY26's 24% revenue growth was 22.3 points volume — the configuration that indicates market expansion rather than mix engineering.
  • Structural margin advantage. 46.5% gross margin against a 39.9% peer median, sustained across a full commodity cycle.
  • Working capital funds itself. A −44 day cash conversion cycle means growth releases cash instead of consuming it.
  • Capacity expansion is funded and dated. INR 958 crore of brownfield capex lifts capacity 43% by H2FY28 without touching the balance sheet.

Risks

  • Capacity ceiling through FY27. Utilisation at 100% caps near-term volume growth at single digits regardless of demand.
  • Competitive entry into 350cc. Five manufacturers have launched into the core segment since FY24; the 88% share is the number most at risk.
  • Energy-linked input inflation. The gross-margin advantage is partly cyclical.
  • Treasury dependence. Other income at 17.9% of PBT flatters headline returns.

What would change this view

Three observable things would break the thesis, in order of how early they would show:

  1. Domestic mid-size share falling below 82% for two consecutive quarters — the point at which competitive entry is taking volume rather than expanding the segment.
  2. Gross margin below 43% for a full year without a corresponding commodity move, indicating the pricing power has gone.
  3. Cheyyar commissioning slipping past Q4FY28, which pushes the volume story out a full year and invalidates the FY29 forecast the DCF rests on.
